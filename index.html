Words Are Not Motions:
Steering Vision-Language-Action Models with Jacobian Pullbacks

The semantic direction that names a motion is not the one that executes it.

Abstract

Just as humans formulate an intention to act before committing muscles to movement, vision-language-action (VLA) models must bridge high-level semantic reasoning with low-level physical control. Yet how conceptual goals translate into motor commands across intermediate representations remains opaque.

Current steering methods typically isolate internal features by the vocabulary tokens they promote, implicitly assuming that the semantic representation naming a motion concept is the one that executes it. In this work, we refute this assumption by extending the Jacobian lens from discrete vocabulary logits to arbitrary differentiable objectives over continuous action trajectories. Our formulation computes vector-Jacobian pullbacks through the policy's downstream dynamics in a single backward pass, providing a training-free, probe-free mechanism that operates at inference time across both autoregressive heads and iterative flow-matching action experts.

Evaluating across OpenVLA and \(\pi_{0.5}\) in simulation and on a physical manipulator reveals a fundamental double dissociation: action-grounded directions reliably modulate trajectory dynamics, including displacement, velocity, and smoothness, while preserving task completion, whereas vocabulary-aligned directions fail at physical control and only influence non-physical semantic choices such as target object selection. Mechanistic analysis reveals the geometric origin of this divergence: linguistic subspaces embed action antonyms as proximate semantic neighbors, whereas action-grounded subspaces map them to opposed vectors that phase-lock to gripper kinematics over time. Our findings demonstrate that effective VLA control requires intervening directly on the internal subspace governing physical execution rather than the linguistic features that label it.

Contributions

  • Causally grounded steering vectors via native Jacobian pullbacks, without auxiliary training (SAEs, linear probes) or manual neuron curation.
  • Zero-shot behavioral steering across discrete (OpenVLA) and continuous flow-matching (\(\pi_{0.5}\)) VLA architectures, in simulated rollouts and real-world tabletop manipulation.
  • Geometric disentanglement of semantic and motor subspaces, showing that activating overlapping subspaces outperforms isolated semantic or kinematic steering.

1Method

A VLA maps images and an instruction to robot commands through a vision-language backbone. We want to find, inside that backbone, residual directions and MLP units that carry a motion concept such as up or slow, and to change the policy's behavior by editing them at inference time without touching the weights. The method has three parts: a Jacobian-lens pullback that turns any scalar readout of the policy's output into a direction at every layer; two families of readouts, linguistic and physical, that give semantic and motor directions; and a residual-swap intervention.

Jacobian pullback

Let \(h^{(\ell)}\in\mathbb R^{P\times d}\) be the residual stream after block \(\ell\) over \(P\) prefix positions. The logit lens reads an intermediate residual by projecting it onto the output vocabulary, and prior VLA steering selects neurons by the tokens their write vectors promote. Both ignore everything downstream of layer \(\ell\): later blocks, normalization, and, for \(\pi_{0.5}\), a flow-matching action expert that has no vocabulary at all. We instead ask how the policy's output responds to a perturbation at layer \(\ell\).

Let \(J\) be a scalar readout of the model output \(z\), either a logit score or a statistic of the sampled action chunk, and let \(\mathcal S\) be a set of source positions. Adding one shared perturbation \(\delta\) to the residual at every position in \(\mathcal S\) after block \(\ell\) and differentiating at \(\delta=0\) gives

$$ g^{(\ell)}_{\mathcal S}=\nabla_\delta J\big(h^{(\ell)}+\mathbf 1_{\mathcal S}\delta^\top\big)\Big|_{\delta=0} =\sum_{p\in\mathcal S}\Big(\frac{\partial z}{\partial h^{(\ell)}_p}\Big)^{\!\top}\nabla_zJ, \qquad \hat g^{(\ell)}=\frac{\bar g^{(\ell)}}{\|\bar g^{(\ell)}\|_2}, $$

where \(\bar g^{(\ell)}\) averages the raw gradient over \(N\) calibration frames. The right-hand side is a vector-Jacobian product through the exact downstream computation, so one backward pass yields a direction at every layer, and each direction inherits whatever nonlinearity lies between layer \(\ell\) and the output. Two source scopes are used: S2 perturbs only the final valid prefix position; S3 perturbs every valid image and text position. A neuron is ranked for a direction by the signed cosine of its MLP write vector with \(\hat g^{(\ell)}\).

Semantic pullbacks

For a concept \(c\), let \(\mathcal T_c\) be a set of single-token synonyms and \(\mathcal C\) a centering vocabulary. The centered readout vector and the \(\pi_{0.5}\) objective are

$$ u_c=\frac1{|\mathcal T_c|}\sum_{t\in\mathcal T_c}E_t-\frac1{|\mathcal C|}\sum_{t\in\mathcal C}E_t,\qquad J^{\mathrm{sem}}_c=\frac1{|\mathcal V|}\sum_{p\in\mathcal V}\big\langle u_c,\operatorname{RMSNorm}\big(h^{(L-1)}_p\big)\big\rangle, $$

where \(E_t\) is the output embedding of token \(t\). \(\pi_{0.5}\) never decodes language, so \(J^{\mathrm{sem}}_c\) probes how strongly the final backbone state promotes \(c\) over the background. Differentiating it gives the semantic J-lens direction; normalizing \(u_c\) directly, with no Jacobian, gives the static semantic logit baseline.

Kinematic pullbacks

A kinematic readout is a differentiable statistic of the predicted commands. For \(\pi_{0.5}\) we fix the sampling noise, run the ten Euler steps of the flow expert on the perturbed prefix, unnormalize the action chunk, and convert it to end-effector displacements \(D_t\in\mathbb R^3\). Three objectives measure the mean vertical displacement, the mean speed, and the mean within-chunk jerk:

$$ J^{dz}=\frac1H\sum_{t=1}^H D_{t,z},\qquad J^{\mathrm{speed}}=\frac1{H\Delta t}\sum_{t=1}^H\|D_t\|_2,\qquad J^{\mathrm{jerk}}=\frac{1}{(H-2)\Delta t^3}\sum_{t=2}^{H-1}\big\|D_{t+1}-2D_t+D_{t-1}\big\|_2 . $$

Their negatives define down, slow, and smooth. Because the expert attends to the prefix cache at every denoising step, the gradient flows through the whole sampler. For OpenVLA, whose actions are discrete tokens, the vertical readout is a centered logit score over the upward or downward action bins. These give the physical J-lens direction. The shared direction is the normalized sum of the unit semantic and physical directions at the same layer and scope.

Feature steering: residual swap

Given unit source and target directions \(\hat d\) and \(\hat u\) at layer \(\ell\), for example down and up or soup and cheese, we edit every position \(p\in\mathcal S\) by

$$ h'_p=h_p+\alpha\,\big[\langle h_p,\hat d\rangle\big]_{+}\,(\hat u-\hat d),\qquad \alpha=1 . $$

The edit is gated by the source: if the state does not encode the source concept, nothing changes. Otherwise it removes a source-directed component and adds an equal coefficient along the target, acting only in \(\mathrm{span}(\hat u,\hat d)\) and leaving the orthogonal complement untouched. For antipodal poles (\(\hat d=-\hat u\)) the swap is a one-sided reflection that preserves the residual norm exactly. Zero-strength and identical-direction edits are exact no-ops and serve as controls. The action expert is never edited.

2Kinematic-level steering

We evaluate \(\pi_{0.5}\) on LIBERO and DROID and OpenVLA on LIBERO. Open-loop evaluation compares steered and unedited predictions on the same held-out frame under identical sampling noise; closed-loop evaluation runs full LIBERO episodes from matched initial states; real-world trials run on a DROID Franka arm. Unless stated otherwise, an intervention is one residual swap at one VLM layer with strength one. Conditions are the semantic, physical, and shared J-lens directions, the static semantic-logit direction, and, where available, the adverb appended to the prompt.

Vertical direction, speed, and jerk are exactly the quantities the kinematic objectives read out. The questions are whether the physical direction survives finite single-layer edits, closed-loop execution, and a real robot; whether the semantic direction for the same word (up, slow, smooth) moves the robot at all; and whether the shared direction helps.

Vertical direction

For vertical swaps we report the expected-direction ratio \(R_z=100\,\mathbb E\big[s\,\Delta v_z/\|\Delta\mathbf v\|_2\big]\), where \(\Delta\mathbf v\) is the steered-minus-baseline velocity command and \(s=\pm1\) for up and down: \(+100\) means every change points the intended way.

In \(\pi_{0.5}\) LIBERO the layer-averaged ratio is 93.8% for physical and 83.7% for shared up→down steering, against 10.1% for semantic J-lens and −9.2% for semantic logit; down→up gives 81.6%, 83.4%, and 7.5%. DROID reproduces this on another checkpoint and embodiment (physical 81.6% / 84.8%, shared 67.0% / 82.3%, semantic within two points of zero). OpenVLA is more layer-dependent and weaker overall (shared 30.4% / 36.9%, physical 30.1% / 27.2%), and its semantic J-lens is the instructive case: 33.1% for down→up but −24.7% for up→down, so the word-aligned direction sends the robot the wrong way for one of two opposite swaps.

Kinematic-grounded directions are the reliable route to directional control; the semantic component adds robustness in some settings but never rescues semantic-only steering.

Speed and smoothness

In open loop, physical and shared directions give the largest changes across most layers, while semantic J-lens moves commanded speed by under 1.5% in every condition. Averaged over DROID layers, shared fast→slow reduces speed by 15.7% (physical 13.3%), shared slow→fast raises it by 12.1% (physical 9.2%), and jerky→smooth lowers jerk by 15.9% for physical and 12.0% for shared; in LIBERO physical slowing is larger (4.2% versus 1.8%). These summarize separate single-layer edits, and the sweeps retain marked outliers such as LIBERO physical layer 16 (see Appendix A.1).

Real-world DROID motion steering

On the real robot, shared steering raises mean EEF speed from 315 to 436 mm/s (+38%) and physical to 330 mm/s (+5%), while the "fast" prompt and both semantic conditions are slower than baseline; the recorded smoothness metric drops by 27% for physical and 24% for shared steering against 19% for the prompt. These are five-trial means without success annotations, so they show transfer of the kinematic edit to hardware, not preserved success. Per-trial values are in Appendix A.5.

3Semantic-level steering

Which object to pick has no kinematic readout: no displacement or speed objective separates cheese from soup, or a red cube from a blue one. Physical and shared directions therefore do not exist for this axis, and the intervention must act through the semantic subspace. We hold the instruction fixed and apply one semantic J-lens swap between two nouns or two colors.

In simulation we build a LIBERO scene-2 wildcard task, "Put * in the basket", whose success predicate accepts any designated object, and swap soup→cheese at one layer on ten matched seeds. The unedited policy picks cheese in 3/10 trials. Swaps at layer 12 or 14 raise this to 6/10 with 10/10 success, while layers 6, 11, and 15 leave it at 3/10, so the effect depends on the layer. On the real robot, with the instruction "pick the cube into the bowl" and one red and one blue cube, a blue→red swap at layer 14 raises red choices from 2/10 to 6/10 across two mirrored layouts. The recorded labels measure choice, not placement success.

Object choice is a language-defined decision, and the semantic subspace alone steers it: in simulation without loss of success, and on hardware with color-defined targets.

4Why steering works: locating the two subspaces

The behavioral results show a division of labor. We ask what in the unedited representations produces it: where each family is expressed across depth, how the geometry of opposite concepts differs, whether features track movement in time, and whether single neurons are causally sufficient.

Depth

Physical J-lens projections rise sharply in the final layers of OpenVLA while semantic projections stay small throughout, so the kinematic direction is expressed near the output. The two families are not independent across depth: pulling late physical directions back to earlier layers in \(\pi_{0.5}\) aligns them systematically with the semantic directions native to those layers (Appendix B.3). Projection magnitude measures expression, not causal importance.

Geometry

In \(\pi_{0.5}\) LIBERO, semantic selections are positively aligned at all 18 layers (mean cosine 0.47 to 0.56 across source scopes), physical selections are opposed at every defined layer (mean −0.19), and shared selections are near orthogonal (\(|\cos|\le0.12\)). The semantic subspace encodes up and down as related concepts; the kinematic subspace encodes them as opposing effects. This is why a word-aligned edit can be weak or wrong-signed, as in the OpenVLA sweep above, and why the shared direction does not inherit the conflation.

Time

The physical up neuron is positive during both lifts, the physical down neuron is positive on the first descent and negative on the first lift, and the shared down neuron is negative while the object is carried and positive on the second descent; the semantic traces are broad and weakly phase-locked. Kinematic features rise and fall with the movement, whereas semantic features are present throughout.

5Conclusion

We adapted the Jacobian lens to VLAs by pulling two kinds of readout back into the backbone residual stream: a language readout of a concept word and a Cartesian readout of the policy's own predicted commands, differentiated through the complete action sampler. The resulting semantic and motor directions behave differently and are useful for different things. Quantities measured on the end effector, namely vertical direction, speed, and smoothness, respond to motor-grounded directions across three checkpoints, two simulators, and a real robot, while word-aligned directions alone are weak or wrong-signed. Object choice, which has no Cartesian readout, responds to semantic directions alone.

The mechanistic analyses explain the split: motor directions are expressed near the output and encode opposite motions as opposed vectors, whereas semantic directions encode opposite words as related ones. Steering therefore works when the edit lives in the subspace that controls the targeted behavior, and co-activating both often helps. Limitations include small real-world samples, coupled speed and jerk, and local-sensitivity directions that do not guarantee closed-loop effects.

B.1 Single-neuron clamp experiment

Causality. Clamping one selected layer-6 neuron gives, at clamp 32, the largest intended command change for the shared selection on every concept (+0.40 ± 0.14 mm up, −0.45 ± 0.17 mm down, +6.9 ± 2.3 mm/s fast), with physical next and semantic-only weaker or wrong-signed for down and fast. The ordering matches the residual-swap results at the level of single units. Together, the four analyses support complementary semantic and kinematic subspaces as the basis for steering, without establishing a complete causal circuit from language to motion.

Protocol and operator definitions

Each condition overwrites one selected post-gating MLP neuron at every valid prefix position before the down projection; a separate unedited run provides the paired baseline. Clamp values are {0, 0.5, 1, 2, 4, 8, 16, 32}, so the zero-clamp condition suppresses the neuron and must not be used as the unedited control. Evaluation uses 75 demonstrations with identical paired noise, 10-step denoising, and 10-action chunks; the \(y\)-axis is 50× the mean clipped \(z\)-action in mm for up/down and 1000× the mean norm of the clipped \(xyz\)-action in mm/s at 20 Hz for fast. Selected indices (zero-based): up: semantic 6161 (J-lens), 6589 (logit), physical 5111, shared 12834; down: 7046, 12486, 6037, 2312; fast: 1406, 1247, 14037, 3727.

For a neuron activation \(a_i\) and write vector \(w_i\), clamp, multiplication and addition would induce the direct changes

$$ \Delta h_{\rm clamp}=(\kappa-a_i)w_i,\qquad \Delta h_{\rm multiply}=(\beta-1)a_iw_i,\qquad \Delta h_{\rm add}=\gamma w_i . $$

Their no-op settings differ (\(\beta=1\), \(\gamma=0\)), whereas a fixed clamp is not generally a no-op. These identities define the operators; a matched comparison with residual swap would require the same observations, sampling noise, layer, edit scope and a documented strength-matching rule, which the existing clamp and swap plots do not supply.

B.2 What the mechanistic figures measure

Figure details for Figures 5a, 5b, and 6

The up/down geometry figure (5b) averages the raw BF16 MLP write vectors within each selected top-ten neuron set before taking their cosine, with no per-neuron normalization, score weighting, or sign flip. Rankings are by cosine with the J-lens semantic, logit-lens semantic, and J-lens \(dz\) directions; the shared curve uses the first ten neurons of the expanding rank intersection. Exact opposition of the physical objective gradients therefore need not imply exact opposition of the mean selected write vectors.

The OpenVLA residual-projection figure (5a) averages absolute projections of unedited post-block states onto unit directions over all expanded-prompt positions plus preceding generated tokens, uniformly across the seven action predictions of 100 unsteered greedy observations. J-lens \(dz\) projections peak at layer 31, logit-lens \(dz\) projections grow steadily, and semantic projections remain small at every layer. Shaded bands are observation standard-error intervals. Larger projections indicate expression of a direction, not by themselves its causal importance.

C. Implementation details

Calibration, vocabularies, and scopes

Directions are fitted on \(N=400\) calibration observations with the task's own instruction and native inputs. For \(\pi_{0.5}\) LIBERO, 40 observations per task are selected deterministically from a 1900-frame pool over the ten LIBERO-10 tasks; evaluation batches contain 100 observations from different demonstrations, disjoint from fitting demonstrations. For \(\pi_{0.5}\) DROID, four observations are taken from each of 100 episodes; its 100 evaluation frames are distinct frames from these same episodes, so DROID is frame-disjoint but not episode-held-out. The original OpenVLA physical bank uses all 1900 frames. For each observation we cache the forward pass, introduce an independent zero-valued edit variable after every block, evaluate the readout, and obtain all per-layer gradients in one reverse pass. Raw gradients are averaged over observations before per-layer normalization; \(\pi_{0.5}\) fitting runs in FP32 with FP64 accumulation.

Target sets contain only tokenizer-verified single-token entries. The reported \(\pi_{0.5}\) motion directions use historical 128/129-token centers, the reported OpenVLA directions a 359-token center, and the LIBERO soup→cheese noun directions are centered over token IDs 0–31999. Vector banks fitted with different centers are not interchangeable. A post-block edit at the final \(\pi_{0.5}\) prefix layer 17 cannot affect a later prefix cache, so steering sweeps exclude that layer.

DROID command geometry and OpenVLA action readout

For LIBERO, \(D_t=0.05\operatorname{clip}(A_t^{xyz},-1,1)\) metres with \(\Delta t=0.05\) s and \(H=10\). For DROID, with observed joints \(q\) and predicted joint commands \(v_t\),

$$ \Delta q_t=\frac{0.2v_t}{\max(1,\|v_t\|_\infty)},\qquad D_t=\operatorname{FK}(q+\Delta q_t)-\operatorname{FK}(q),\qquad \Delta t=1/15\ \mathrm{s},\ H=15, $$

where each prediction is referenced independently to the observed \(q\) rather than integrated. OpenVLA discretizes each of its seven action coordinates into 256 uniform bins; its vertical readout is the centered mean logit over the upward (or downward) bin set of coordinate index 2, each polarity centered separately, so the up and down readouts are not exact negatives of one another.

Euler flow-matching backpropagation

The \(\pi_{0.5}\) action expert integrates a learned velocity field \(v_\theta\) conditioned on the prefix key-value cache \(\mathrm{KV}(\delta)\). With fixed initial noise and \(\tau_k=1-k/K\), the Euler updates are \(x_{k+1}=x_k-\frac1K v_\theta(x_k,\tau_k;\mathrm{KV}(\delta))\) with \(K=10\), and reverse-mode differentiation through the unrolled solver gives

$$ \frac{\partial J}{\partial\delta}=-\frac1K\sum_{k=0}^{K-1}\lambda_{k+1}^{\top}\frac{\partial v_\theta(x_k,\tau_k;\mathrm{KV}(\delta))}{\partial\delta},\qquad \lambda_K=\nabla_{x_K}J,\quad \lambda_k=\lambda_{k+1}-\frac1K\Big(\frac{\partial v_\theta}{\partial x_k}\Big)^{\!\top}\lambda_{k+1}. $$

Because the expert attends to the prefix cache at every step, every term depends on \(\delta\), and the gradient includes the full sampler and the action unnormalization.

Residual-swap properties

Write \(a=h^\top s\), \(b=h^\top t\), \(c=s^\top t\) with unit \(s,t\). If \(a\le0\), the swap leaves \(h\) unchanged (context-conditionality). For \(a>0\) and \(\alpha=1\), \(h'^\top s=ac\), \(h'^\top t=b+a(1-c)\), and \(\|h'\|_2^2-\|h\|_2^2=2a(b-ac)\): the target projection does not decrease, the source projection generally remains nonzero, and the norm can change. Any vector orthogonal to both \(s\) and \(t\) has identical inner product with \(h\) and \(h'\) (subspace preservation). When \(t=-s\), the swap is a one-sided Householder reflection \(h'=h+2[-\langle h,\hat u\rangle]_+\hat u\) and the norm is preserved exactly; for general strength the norm difference is \(4\alpha(\alpha-1)a^2\), preserved at strengths zero and one.

Closed-loop randomization protocols

Adverb rollouts use matched initial states and noise seeds derived from a hash of the trial seed and replanning index. Wildcard object trials use seeded native resets and native policy RNG splitting. Both predict ten commands and execute five before replanning, with ten settling steps and a 520-step limit. These protocols are separate and are not pooled. Real-world measurements come from the experiment workbook rather than from the simulation controller.

D. Limitations and reproducibility

The Jacobian directions are averages of local sensitivities. Finite-strength interventions can change attention, activate nonlinearities, and alter subsequent observations, so an objective-aligned derivative is not a guarantee of a desired closed-loop effect. Object choice is evaluated on a small shared seed set and real-world measurements have small sample sizes. DROID open-loop evaluation is frame-disjoint but uses the calibration episodes. Speed and jerk are coupled, and the reported experiments do not isolate smoothness at fixed speed. The historical clamp study does not establish an operator ranking against addition, multiplication, or residual swap. No magnitude filtering or exclusion of clipped open-loop values is used in the reported results. Code, frozen numeric summaries, and the plotting scripts will be released after the review period.
